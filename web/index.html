At the Moon's south pole the Sun and Earth skim the horizon, so the hills and crater rims around a landing site decide whether it has power and a link home. Four Lights checks 12 candidate sites against their real skylines, from NASA's laser-altimeter terrain, for every hour of 2028 and 2029.

Start here: why the south pole is different

The Sun never climbs

The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5°, so at its poles the Sun never rises high or sets for long: it circles the horizon once every 29.5 Earth days, always within a degree or two of it. A hill in the way means night. A high rim can stay sunlit for months.

Earth sits on the horizon too

Radio travels in straight lines, so a lander needs Earth in view to talk home. From the pole Earth hangs low in one part of the sky and bobs up and down by about 6° either way over 27 days. This is called libration: the Moon's tilt and its orbit make it nod toward and away from us. Our data includes it, because Earth's position comes from NASA's JPL Horizons for every hour. Terrain can hide Earth for a fortnight at a time.

Why go there at all

Crater floors near the pole have not seen sunlight for billions of years and are among the coldest places measured in the Solar System. Water ice has been detected there. The best sites sit on bright high ground next to that permanent shadow.

Why the south pole, and not the north

Both poles have the low Sun and craters in permanent shadow. The south has more shadowed ground, in bigger and deeper craters, because it sits on the rim of the South Pole–Aitken basin, the largest and oldest impact basin known on the Moon. That rough ground puts sunlit ridges right beside the shadow, and the basin's ancient rock is a science prize of its own. The south also has the direct evidence: in 2009 NASA's LCROSS mission crashed a spent rocket stage into Cabeus crater there and found water in the plume it threw up. All of NASA's Artemis candidate landing regions are near the south pole.

    Sun-Path Chart

    How to read this chart

    • Imagine standing at the site and turning all the way round. Left to right is the direction you face (north, east, south, west); up and down is how high above level ground you look, in degrees. Your fist at arm's length covers about 10°.
    • The grey shape is the real skyline: the highest ground in each direction, measured by a laser on NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter.
    • The gold line is the Sun's path over the mission and the blue line is Earth's. Where a line passes behind the grey it fades: that body is hidden from the site. Drag the time slider and watch.
    • At the south pole, "north" is every direction away from the pole, and south is the pole itself.

      What the four lights mean

      • Sun: can you see any of the Sun right now?
      • Earth: can you see Earth right now?
      • Power: over the whole stay, is there enough sunlight to run on solar panels, with no night too long for the batteries?
      • Comms: is Earth clear of the hills, and is one of NASA's three big dish stations (in California, Spain and Australia) facing the Moon?

      About this site

      Where AI is used on this page, and where it isn't

      Not for the answers

      The skylines, the Sun and Earth positions, the four lights and the planner are ordinary calculation on NASA data. The same inputs always give the same answer, and you can check each step. No AI model is involved.

      For writing the site reviews

      A language model works by predicting which words are likely to come next. That makes it good at writing a clear sentence and bad at guaranteeing the sentence is true. So we give it only a table of facts we computed for the site, and a separate program checks every number in what it writes. Drafts that fail are thrown away.

      For understanding your question

      When you type a question, the model's only job is to pull out four settings: a date, a slip in days, a mission length and a site. The server checks each one is allowed, and then the planner does the calculating. The model never sees the data.

      It did get something wrong

      While building this, one review said a site's terrain data was "approximate" when it was not. Every number in the paragraph was right, so the number check passed it. We added a second check for that word, and on the next run it rejected six drafts. The lesson: a model's mistakes can be in the words between the numbers, and you only catch what you think to check for.

      Try it

      • Ask the question box something unrelated, such as "what is the capital of France?" It should refuse, because that is not a date, a slip, a window or a site.
      • Ask "we are delayed a week". Notice that it keeps your current date and adds seven days: the model supplied the 7, and the planner did the rest.
      • Read a site review beside the figures under "About this site". Can you find anything the review says that the figures do not support?

      Things to try

      1. Slip the launch. In the Slip Planner, press "Slip + 1 day" three times. Which sites change colour? Open one that went red and find the night that caused it.
      2. Stand taller. In the Sun-Path Chart, change the observer height from 1 m to 5 m. Why does a few metres change the skyline nearby but not the far ridges?
      3. Compare two sites. Look at Shackleton rim and Haworth for the same week. One has more sunlight, the other sees Earth more. Which would you choose for a solar-powered rover, and which for a radio relay?
      4. Size a battery. Under Survive the Night, open "Adjust the power system" and find the smallest battery that gets the small lander through the longest night of the week.
      5. Find the best fortnight. Set the mission window to 14 days and slide through the year. When is the best time to land at Connecting Ridge?

      Words used here

      Azimuth
      The direction you face, in degrees clockwise from north.
      Elevation
      How high something is above level ground, in degrees. The horizon is 0°.
      Horizon profile (skyline)
      The elevation of the highest ground in every direction from one spot.
      Libration
      The slow nodding of the Moon as seen from Earth, which makes Earth bob up, down and sideways in the Moon's sky over about 27 days. It is built into the Earth positions used here.
      Mission window
      The days a lander is working on the surface, starting at the landing date.
      Usable days
      Time in the mission window with both sunlight and a radio link to Earth.
      Deep Space Network (DSN)
      NASA's three stations of large radio dishes, spaced round the Earth so one can always face deep space.
      LOLA
      The Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter, which has measured the Moon's shape with billions of laser shots.
